## Q1: What taxonomies of cross-platform approaches exist?

### Takeaway
The literature converges on a small set of runtime-based versus generative categories. They are native as the baseline, (mobile) Web apps, Hybrid (WebView wrapper), Interpreted (on-device JS engine plus native UI), Cross-compiled, and Model-Driven, and PWAs were added as a new category around 2017/2018. The main disagreement is where to place specific frameworks: Xamarin, and later Flutter, is sometimes "interpreted" and sometimes "cross-compiled". El-Kassas et al. offer a finer-grained, more technical taxonomy.

### Cited Findings

**Heitkötter, Hanschke & Majchrzak (2012/2013). The first widely used evaluation framework**
- They proposed a criteria catalogue with two perspectives, applied to mobile Web apps, PhoneGap (Cordova) and Titanium Mobile, and native Android/iOS as a benchmark. Main verdict: "we specifically found PhoneGap viable if very close resemblance of a native look & feel can be neglected." — [Heitkötter et al. 2012, WEBIST PDF](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2012/39045/39045.pdf)
- **Infrastructure perspective** criteria: I1 License and costs; I2 Supported platforms; I3 Access to advanced device-specific features (NFC, accelerometer, multi-touch); I4 Long-term feasibility; I5 Look and feel (including app life-cycle, "instant on", resuming after interruption); I6 Application speed (start-up and runtime, assessed subjectively, not measured); I7 Distribution (app stores vs. other channels, updates). — [Heitkötter et al. 2012](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2012/39045/39045.pdf)
- **Development perspective** criteria: D1 Development environment; D2 GUI design; D3 Ease of development (docs, learning curve); D4 Maintainability (measured by LOC); D5 Scalability; D6 Opportunities for further development (code reuse across approaches, lock-in); D7 Speed and cost of development. — [Heitkötter et al. 2012](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2012/39045/39045.pdf)
- On Web apps: "Distributing a Web app is simple. Users only need to know its URL and they will automatically get the most recent version. Using app stores is generally not possible." Launch "may be slow" because the app loads over the Internet. The Web-app prototype took the shortest development time of all approaches, and "A project started as a Web app can easily be ported to PhoneGap if access to the native API should become necessary." — [Heitkötter et al. 2012](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2012/39045/39045.pdf)
- Discussion: "cross-platform approaches are a viable alternative. As soon as mobile apps have to be developed for multiple platforms under tight budgets, with small developer teams, and in a short time frame, a cross-platform approach is necessary." — [Heitkötter et al. 2012](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2012/39045/39045.pdf)
- Follow-ups by the same group: a weighted evaluation framework (Rieger & Majchrzak 2016, LNBIP 232, pp. 18–39, cited in HICSS 2018 ref. [8]). A later JSS 2019 paper proposes "what could become the definitive framework for evaluating cross-platform approaches", with weighted criteria covering devices beyond smartphones (PC–tablet hybrids, wearables). It compares frameworks against Web Apps and native and concludes that "cross-platform development has seen much progress but the challenges are ever growing." — [Rieger & Majchrzak 2019, JSS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jss.2019.04.001)

**Raj & Tolety (2012)**
- The abstract frames cross-platform tools as helping "to develop the applications for multiple platforms in less time", notes that "cross platform application development is also not seamless, it has its own fallacies and pitfalls", and says the paper aims to give information "to make a suitable choice" (criteria for selecting an approach). — [Raj & Tolety 2012, IEEE INDICON](https://doi.org/10.1109/INDCON.2012.6420693)
- See Gaps: the four-way classification commonly attributed to this paper (Web / Hybrid / Interpreted / Cross-compiled) could not be checked against the full text.

**El-Kassas, Abdullah, Yousef & Wahba (2017). A finer technical taxonomy**
- The abstract says earlier surveys cover "Cross-Compilation approach, Virtual Machine approach, and Web-Based approach" but not "the most recent approaches, including Component-Based approach, Cloud-Based approach, and Merged approach". The paper "introduces a comprehensive categorization to the cross-platform approaches, defines the pros and cons of each approach, explains sample solutions per approach, compares the cross-platform mobile development solutions, and ends with the open research areas." — [El-Kassas et al. 2017, Ain Shams Eng. J.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asej.2015.08.004)
- Biørn-Hansen et al. describe it as a taxonomy that illustrates "low-level differences between development approaches in a technical matter". They note that El-Kassas et al. place **Xamarin in the Interpreted approach** (together with Titanium), while Willocx et al. place it in Cross-compiled. Biørn-Hansen et al. side with Cross-compiled because Xamarin compiles C# to native byte code. — [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018, ACM CSUR (author PDF)](http://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/16569/1/Fulltext.pdf)

**Biørn-Hansen, Grønli & Ghinea (2018, ACM Computing Surveys). The five-category taxonomy most useful for the thesis**
- The "pool of common cross-platform approaches includes Hybrid, Interpreted, Cross-compiled and Model-Driven development ... with a novel approach coined Progressive Web Apps also on the rise." Table 1 lists frameworks per approach. — [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018](http://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/16569/1/Fulltext.pdf)
  - **Hybrid**: Cordova, Capacitor, PhoneGap, Ionic Framework, Onsen UI, Framework7, Quasar, Sencha Touch, and others.
  - **Interpreted**: React Native, NativeScript, Tabris.js, Titanium Appcelerator, Weex, and others.
  - **Cross-compiled**: Xamarin, **Flutter**, Codename One, Qt Mobile, RAD Studio, and others.
  - **Model-Driven**: MD2, MobML, Applause, Mendix, WebRatio, XIS-Mobile, and others.
  - **PWA**: implemented with ordinary web frameworks (Ionic, Vue.js, Angular, React.js, Svelte, Preact, Polymer, Stencil, and others).
- **Hybrid**: a native project containing a WebView ("simply put, an embeddable web browser") plus JS↔native communication code, also called "Native-wrapper". Access to device features goes through **bridging** (a Foreign Function Interface) and Cordova's plugin system (camera, GPS, contacts). Apps are "installable from any typical mobile app store." — [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018](http://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/16569/1/Fulltext.pdf)
- **Interpreted**: apps "do not rely on a WebView component"; they "render actual Native user interface components", enabled by "on-device JavaScript interpreters". JavaScriptCore is the default on iOS, and V8 is frequently used on Android. They also use bridging (FFI) to reach device features. No "Cordova for Interpreted apps" exists, so plugins are framework-specific and the ecosystem is fragmented. — [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018](http://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/16569/1/Fulltext.pdf)
- **Cross-compiled**: no WebView and no on-device interpreter. "a common language such as C# (Xamarin) is compiled to Native byte code"; "the bridging layer known from e.g. the Interpreted and Hybrid approaches does not exist". — [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018](http://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/16569/1/Fulltext.pdf)
- **Flutter** (discussed as an emerging technology in 2018): "a reactive framework for building cross-platform apps without the need of an interpreter or WebView". It uses AOT compilation of Dart, and "implements their own set of interface widgets". — [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018](http://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/16569/1/Fulltext.pdf)
- **Model-Driven**: a DSL plus generators that produce native source. The approach is common in academia but "rare among practitioners". — [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018](http://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/16569/1/Fulltext.pdf)
- **PWA**: "At its core, a PWA is a web app with enhanced capabilities", served from a web server at a URL. It installs to the home screen, works offline, and runs in an "artifact-less" browser window configured by a manifest. It is built from Service Workers, a JSON manifest and an Application Shell. The Service Worker handles lifecycle, data synchronisation and push notifications. PWAs still lack "access to most if not all of platform and device features". Service Workers arrived on iOS in 11.3, with limitations. — [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018](http://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/16569/1/Fulltext.pdf)
- The survey's state-of-research matrix (Table 6) covers approaches × core concepts: user experience, device/platform features, performance, and security. The authors conclude that the field needs "empirical verification of an array of unbacked claims" and more qualitative, user-oriented research. — [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1145/3241739)

**Majchrzak, Biørn-Hansen & Grønli (2018, HICSS-51). PWAs placed in the taxonomy**
- Figure 1 ("Categorization of Cross-Platform Approaches", adapted from Majchrzak et al. 2015 and Heitkötter et al. 2012) splits cross-platform development into two paradigms:
  - **Runtime environments**: Mobile Webapps (no native elements), Hybrids (native packaging), Self-contained runtime (native GUI).
  - **Generative approaches**: MDSD (compiled to native), Transpiling (uses native SDK).
  - Native, platform-specific development sits outside cross-platform. — [Majchrzak et al. 2018](http://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/50607/1/paper0720.pdf)
- "Hybrid apps typically rely on Web technology but provide a native packaging ... [this] limited these apps to a look & feel that at most approximated native apps. Finally, apps based on a self-contained runtime are similar to hybrids yet use native GUI elements." — [Majchrzak et al. 2018](http://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/50607/1/paper0720.pdf)
- The authors argue that PWAs had not been considered a cross-platform approach before, "although they by concept seek to support multiple platforms from one code base", and that "only with PWAs an extension [of the categorization] might soon be necessary". — [Majchrzak et al. 2018](http://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/50607/1/paper0720.pdf)
- Their answer to the title question is cautious. Whether PWAs "fulfil many requirements for unified multi-platform development already, is too early to say", and it is "very unlikely that PWAs will be a unifier if iOS-based" devices lack support. In mid-2017 Safari did not implement Service Workers, so "no iOS devices are able to take full advantage of PWAs". The authors mention speculation that this was linked to App Store revenue. — [Majchrzak et al. 2018](http://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/50607/1/paper0720.pdf)
- Industry numbers cited in the paper (secondary, reported by Google): Twitter's native apps were >23 MiB (Android) and >100 MiB (iOS), versus 0.6 MiB for the Twitter Lite PWA. OLA's PWA was 0.2 MiB, versus 60 MiB (Android) and 100 MiB (iOS) native. — [Majchrzak et al. 2018](http://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/50607/1/paper0720.pdf)

### Inferences
- For the thesis, the cleanest scheme to use is Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018's five categories plus native and plain Web as baselines. It names PWA explicitly and places React Native (Interpreted) and Flutter (Cross-compiled) directly. The HICSS runtime-vs-generative split can be cited as the higher-level view.
- Course Hub combines two categories from these taxonomies: a **PWA** (Next.js) and an **Interpreted** app (React Native/Expo). They share TypeScript code, which the taxonomies do not treat as a separate approach. It is a "merged" strategy in the loose sense of El-Kassas et al.; whether their "Merged approach" means exactly this could not be checked (see Gaps).
- Flutter's category is debated. In 2018 the CSUR classified it as Cross-compiled, but its defining trait is its own rendering engine (see Q2/Q3, Flutter docs). Newer papers such as Oliveira et al. 2023 describe it as "compiled and run fully natively".

### Gaps
- **Raj & Tolety 2012**: only the abstract was accessible (IEEE Xplore did not render). The commonly cited four categories (Web, Hybrid, Interpreted, Cross-compiled) and their selection criteria could **not** be verified in the full text. Cite the paper only for its general framing, or check a library copy.
- **El-Kassas et al. 2017**: ScienceDirect returned 403 (the article is open access, so the user can open it). The sub-approaches (e.g. Compilation → cross-compiler / trans-compiler; Interpretation → web-based / virtual machine / runtime interpretation; Modeling; Cloud-based; Merged), the pros/cons table, and the exact framework placements were not verified beyond the abstract and the CSUR remark about Xamarin.
- **Heitkötter et al.**: the full text read was the WEBIST 2012 conference version. The LNBIP 2013 revised chapter (pp. 120–138) was verified only through its metadata; its content may differ slightly.
- The 2017 WEBIST PWA position paper (Biørn-Hansen, Majchrzak, Grønli) was not opened beyond its metadata.

## Q2: What do newer empirical comparisons (2019–2026) report on React Native vs Flutter vs native vs PWA/Web performance and energy?

### Takeaway
Empirical results are mixed and depend heavily on the task. There is no uniform "cross-platform is slower" result.
- **Biørn-Hansen et al. 2020** (bridge/device-API benchmarks on Android): overhead is statistically significant for most frameworks, yet React Native used less task memory than native, and Flutter had the highest idle memory.
- **Oliveira et al. 2023** (CPU-bound benchmarks): Flutter usually had the lowest overhead and React Native the highest, but React Native was best in an animation-heavy app.
- **Horn et al. 2023**: Web apps in the browser use about 53% more energy and about 49% more CPU than native apps.
- **Malavolta et al. 2017**: Service Workers themselves have no significant energy impact.

### Cited Findings

**Biørn-Hansen, Rieger, Grønli, Majchrzak & Ghinea (2020, Empirical Software Engineering)**
- Setup: benchmarks the "bridge enabling cross-platform code to communicate with the underlying operating system and device hardware APIs". There are five cross-platform frameworks plus a native Android baseline: native Java, Ionic (Hybrid), React Native and NativeScript (Interpreted), Flutter, and MAML/MD2 (Model-Driven). Tests ran on six physical Android devices. — [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2020, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10664-020-09827-6); framework list and details from the [Springer article page](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10664-020-09827-6)
- Tasks included device-feature access: accelerometer, geolocation, file-system read of a 613 KB image, and contact insertion. Metrics were time-to-completion (TTC), CPU usage, idle-state memory (PreRAM), active memory (RAM), and task-specific memory (ComputedRAM). This task/metric list comes from a tool summary of the article page, not a verbatim quote, so check it against the PDF. — [Springer article page](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10664-020-09827-6)
- Verbatim results:
  - "In total, n = 16290 individual data points were manually gathered for this study."
  - "Across all tasks, Flutter has the highest idle-state memory usage of the studied frameworks, up to a tenfold increase for the geolocation task compared to native."
  - "React Native undercuts the mean memory usage of the baseline."
  - "Results from the Ionic benchmarks indicate that the framework may cross the 10 000 ms mark for fetching geolocation data more often than the other implementations."
  - For CPU, "MD2 comes out as the winner", with "React Native and Ionic standing out as less effective."
  - "The low values for p < 0.01 for all cross-platform frameworks indicate significance except for MAML/MD2."
  - Source: [Springer article page](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10664-020-09827-6)
- Conclusion (abstract): cross-platform frameworks "may lead to decreased performance compared to the native development approach. Nevertheless, certain cross-platform frameworks can perform equally well or even better than native on certain metrics which highlights the importance of well-defined technical requirements and specifications". — [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10664-020-09827-6)
- Note: the React Native tested was the pre-2024 **old-bridge** architecture (the paper predates the New Architecture).

**Oliveira, Moraes, Castor & Fernandes (2023, EASE)**
- They compared Flutter, React Native and Ionic with functionally equivalent native Java Android variants. The workload was 10 optimised CPU-intensive benchmarks plus two apps, measuring energy, execution time and memory. — [Oliveira et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3593434.3593487)
- "In five of the ten benchmarks, at least one framework-based version exhibits lower energy consumption and execution time than its native counterpart, up to a reduction of 81% in energy and 83% in execution time." In three other benchmarks, framework and native versions performed similarly. — [Oliveira et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3593434.3593487)
- "Overall, Flutter, usually imposes the least overhead in execution time and energy, while React Native imposes the highest in all the benchmarks." However, "in an app that continuously animates multiple images on the screen, without interaction, the React Native version uses the least CPU and energy, up to a reduction of 96% in energy compared to the second-best framework-based version." — [Oliveira et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3593434.3593487)
- Their characterisation: Flutter is "compiled and run fully natively"; React Native "runs interpreted JavaScript code combined with native views"; Ionic "is based on web apps". — [Oliveira et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3593434.3593487)

**Horn et al. (2023, MOBILESoft). Native vs Web versions of the same service**
- Setup: 10 Internet content platforms (e.g. Spotify, YouTube, Reddit) in 5 categories, native Android app versus the Web version in the browser. They measured energy, network traffic, CPU, memory and frame time. — [Horn et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.16734)
- Results:
  - "Web apps consume about 53% more energy than their native counterparts", with a large effect size (Cliff's delta −0.98 for energy).
  - "The CPU utilization of Web apps is roughly 49% higher than their native versions."
  - Memory is higher for Web apps (large effect), which the authors attribute "most likely" to "the overhead posed by the Google Chrome browser".
  - Network traffic difference: small effect. Frame time: no conclusion possible.
  - Source: [Horn et al. 2023 (arXiv full text)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.16734)
- Horn et al.'s related-work section summarises Corbalán et al. (secondary citation): across Web / Hybrid / Interpreted / Cross-compiled frameworks, "Web and Hybrid Approaches consume the most energy, with 3.31% and 3.03% in Android, whilst iOS devices consumed 2.48% and 2.5%." — [Horn et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.16734)

**Malavolta et al. (2017, MOBILESoft). PWA Service Workers and energy**
- Setup: 7 PWAs on two devices (LG G2, Nexus 6P), on 2G and WiFi, with and without Service Workers. Result: "service workers do not have a significant impact over the energy consumption of the two devices, regardless of the network conditions." The authors conclude the PWA/Service-Worker technology "is promising in terms of energy efficiency". — [Malavolta et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1109/MOBILESoft.2017.7)
- A 2020 follow-up evaluates PWA caching's effect on energy and performance; caching is "the main technique used by PWA developers for optimizing network usage and for providing a meaningful experience even when the user's device is offline" (results not retrieved). — [Malavolta et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3387905.3388593)

**Mushtaq, Azam & Anwar (2024, ICSTE). Flutter vs React Native vs Xamarin**
- Using a standard benchmarking app profiled with Android Studio Profiler and Perfetto: "Flutter offers the best performance due to its direct compilation of native code on Android". "React Native has a very low learning curve being dependent on JavaScript which is a famous language among web developers". Xamarin had the easiest setup. The abstract gives no numbers. — [Mushtaq et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICSTE63875.2024.00011)

**Older evidence summarised in the 2018 CSUR (secondary, for context)**
- The "general perception in academia" is that cross-platform performance is "inherently subpar to Native apps due to abstraction layers". Specific studies:
  - Willocx et al. (2016) found "acceptable performance loss", with the penalty dependent on approach/framework and more acceptable on high-end devices. Hybrid page navigation was even more efficient than native.
  - Ahti et al. found a PhoneGap app with faster start-up, lower memory and less disk use than native on Android.
  - Ciman & Gaggi found cross-platform apps carry "some form of an energy consumption penalty".
  - Source: [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018](http://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/16569/1/Fulltext.pdf)

### Inferences
- The evidence does not support a blanket "native always wins" claim for multi-platform apps. The two strongest peer-reviewed studies (EMSE 2020, EASE 2023) both say explicitly that the framework choice should follow the app's workload profile.
- For a course app (lists, video playback, quizzes; mostly I/O-bound, not CPU-bound), the benchmark-level overheads matter less than start-up time, memory and UI smoothness. The EMSE 2020 finding that React Native undercut native task memory is favourable, but it was measured on the old bridge.
- Horn et al.'s 53% energy gap is for **browser-based Web apps**, not installed PWAs specifically, and it comes from comparing different codebases (real-world services). It is still the best recent quantitative evidence that the browser runtime costs more than native.

### Gaps
- **Huber, Demetz & Felderer (2022, Information Systems 108, 102017)** and **(2021, ICWE, LNCS pp. 464–479)**: only metadata was obtainable (ScienceDirect/Springer blocked, and no abstract in OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar). **Do not cite their findings or numbers** until someone opens the abstract. The metadata above is verified.
- No peer-reviewed empirical study was found that benchmarks React Native **with the New Architecture** (≥0.76) against Flutter or native. All academic RN numbers above predate it.
- Suri et al. (2022, ACM ICIMMI, DOI 10.1145/3590837.3590897, "Kotlin, React Native and Flutter"): the abstract has no results, so it is not usable.
- The EMSE 2020 per-task numeric tables (exact ms/MB per framework) were not extracted; only the verbatim summary sentences above were.
- There are no iOS-side numbers in the main studies (EMSE 2020, EASE 2023 and Horn 2023 are all Android-only).

## Q3: How does React Native's New Architecture (JSI, Fabric, TurboModules; default since 0.76) change the classic "bridge" critique?

### Takeaway
The academic critique of the Interpreted approach centres on the JS↔native **bridge** (Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018, 2020). React Native's New Architecture, production-ready and **enabled by default since React Native 0.76 (23 October 2024)**, removes that asynchronous, serialising bridge. It replaces it with JSI (direct, synchronous C++ references), a C++ renderer (Fabric) with synchronous layout and concurrent React support, and lazily loaded, type-safe native modules (TurboModules + Codegen). This undercuts the specific mechanism the 2018–2020 papers criticise, although no independent peer-reviewed benchmark yet confirms the gains.

### Cited Findings
- Old architecture: "React Native communicated with the native platform using an asynchronous bridge. To render a component or call a native function, React Native needed to serialize and enqueue native functions calls with the bridge, which would be processed asynchronously." "Serializing function calls over the bridge quickly became a bottleneck, especially for frequent updates or large objects". The old design also caused visual jumps from intermediate states, and "Layout could only be computed on a single thread". — [React Native blog, "New Architecture is here" (2024-10-23)](https://reactnative.dev/blog/2024/10/23/the-new-architecture-is-here)
- JSI: "JSI is an interface that allows JavaScript to hold a reference to a C++ object and vice-versa. With a memory reference, you can directly invoke methods without serialization costs." Example given: VisionCamera processing ~30 MB frame buffers, "roughly 2 GB of data per second". — [reactnative.dev, About the New Architecture](https://reactnative.dev/architecture/landing-page)
- New Native Modules (TurboModules): "Synchronous access to and from the native runtime", "Type safety between JavaScript and native code", "Code sharing across platforms", "Lazy module loading by default". Codegen defines "a strongly typed contract between the JavaScript layer and the native layer", motivated by the observation that "cross-boundary type errors are one of the most common sources of crashes in cross-platform apps". — [RN blog 2024](https://reactnative.dev/blog/2024/10/23/the-new-architecture-is-here)
- New Renderer (Fabric): "Updates can be rendered on different threads at different priorities"; "Layout can be read synchronously and across different threads"; "The renderer is written in C++ and shared across all platforms". It supports React 18+ concurrent features (Suspense, Transitions, automatic batching) and synchronous `useLayoutEffect` layout reads. — [RN blog 2024](https://reactnative.dev/blog/2024/10/23/the-new-architecture-is-here); [RN architecture page](https://reactnative.dev/architecture/landing-page)
- Bridgeless mode: "we've also fully removed React Native's dependency on the bridge, replacing it with direct, efficient communication between JavaScript and native code using JSI." "Removing the bridge improves startup time by avoiding bridge initialization". The bridge code remains for backward compatibility through an interop layer (with limitations: no custom Shadow Nodes or concurrent features). — [RN blog 2024](https://reactnative.dev/blog/2024/10/23/the-new-architecture-is-here)
- Default status: "Starting from React Native 0.76, the New Architecture is enabled by default in your projects." At 0.76, more than 850 libraries were already compatible, "including all libraries with over 200K weekly downloads". Other 0.76 release notes: Android app size reduced by ~3.8 MB (20%) and Android start-up improved by ~15 ms (~8%). These are vendor-reported figures, and the size/start-up gains come from other 0.76 changes (native library merging), not necessarily from the New Architecture itself. — [RN 0.76 release blog](https://reactnative.dev/blog/2024/10/23/release-0.76-new-architecture); [RN blog 2024](https://reactnative.dev/blog/2024/10/23/the-new-architecture-is-here)
- Academic framing that the New Architecture addresses: in the Interpreted and Hybrid approaches, device access relies on "bridging (Foreign Function Interfaces)", and Cross-compiled approaches are distinguished precisely because the "bridging layer ... does not exist" there. — [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018](http://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/16569/1/Fulltext.pdf). The EMSE 2020 study explicitly benchmarks "the bridge". — [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10664-020-09827-6)

**Contrast: Flutter and Capacitor (official docs), for placing them in the taxonomy**
- Flutter bypasses "the system UI widget libraries in favor of its own widget set. The Dart code that paints Flutter's visuals is compiled into native code, which uses Impeller for rendering." "For release, Flutter apps are compiled directly to machine code ... or to JavaScript if targeting the web." Platform APIs are reached through platform channels (message passing with a codec) or `dart:ffi`, which "can be considerably faster than platform channels, because no serialization is required". Flutter's own framing of OEM-widget frameworks: abstraction layers over native UI libraries add "overhead that can be significant, particularly where there is a lot of interaction between the UI and the app logic." — [Flutter architectural overview](https://docs.flutter.dev/resources/architectural-overview)
- Capacitor (the successor to Cordova, from the Ionic team) is "a cross-platform native runtime that makes it easy to build performant mobile applications that run natively on iOS, Android, and more using modern web tooling". It is web-first ("if it works in the browser, it probably works in a mobile app when using Capacitor") and reaches native APIs through plugins written in Swift and Java. The 2018 survey already notes Capacitor spanning Hybrid, PWA and desktop. — [Capacitor docs](https://capacitorjs.com/docs); [Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018](http://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/16569/1/Fulltext.pdf)

### Inferences
- With JSI replacing the bridge, React Native's architecture now resembles Flutter's FFI path: a direct native call without serialization. The line between "Interpreted" and "Cross-compiled" that the 2018 taxonomy drew by the presence or absence of a bridge is blurring. The JS still runs in an on-device engine (Hermes by default in current RN; not verified here), so React Native remains "Interpreted/JS-runtime" in the taxonomy's terms.
- Pre-2024 empirical results about React Native (EMSE 2020, EASE 2023) should be presented as evidence about the **legacy bridge architecture**. The thesis can argue that their main mechanism of overhead has been re-engineered, while stating that independent peer-reviewed confirmation is still missing.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or independent benchmark of the RN New Architecture was found. All performance claims come from Meta/React Native team posts.
- The Hermes engine as the default JS engine and the Expo SDK's adoption of the New Architecture were not verified in this session. Check the Expo changelog if the thesis relies on them.
